\begin{abstract}
We forecast hourly PM2.5 at 42 monitoring stations across Delhi-NCR over
2019--2025 and ask whether a wind-aware graph provides predictive gains over a
static distance-based graph. On held-out 2024--2025 data the best neural model
(per-station STGCN without a graph, predicting changes from the last observation)
reaches MAE 32.61~$\mu$g/m$^3$, against 44.03 for ridge regression on the last
24 lags and 46.79 for persistence. The graph comparison is judged with a paired
bootstrap over the 105 test weeks rather than a per-seed standard deviation.
For Graph WaveNet the wind-aware graph beats the static distance graph
(33.76 vs.\ 34.39~$\mu$g/m$^3$, difference 0.63, 95\% CI [0.36, 0.91]);
for STGCN the two are indistinguishable (0.12, CI [$-$0.15, 0.41]).
In both architectures, dropping the distance graph is significantly better than
keeping it; dropping the wind-aware graph is also better, significantly so for
STGCN (no graph wins by 0.45, CI [0.15, 0.79]) and not significantly for
Graph WaveNet (0.19, CI [$-$0.15, 0.55]).
The graph learned end-to-end (Graph WaveNet's adaptive adjacency) and the
correlation graph are the two worst configurations; the correlation graph
reaches 35.03~$\mu$g/m$^3$ ($+$1.46 vs.\ no graph, CI [0.70, 2.29]).
A network-mean diagnostic shows that handing the model the network-wide
average explicitly gains essentially nothing (0.01~$\mu$g/m$^3$), so
inter-station mixing is not delivering a useful regional signal.
The consistent reading is that the per-station autoregressive signal dominates
and inter-station mixing is at best a weak positive and at worst a net cost.
Whether the mixing is harmful or just redundant is a hypothesis consistent
with the data, not a demonstrated mechanism.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Air pollution in Delhi-NCR is a severe public health crisis.  Accurate hourly
PM2.5 forecasts at station level could enable targeted interventions.  Recent
literature proposes spatiotemporal graph neural networks (STGNNs) for air
quality forecasting, claiming that graph structure---whether static
(distance, correlation) or wind-aware---captures pollutant transport and
improves predictions.  This work tests that claim on a frozen, real-world
dataset spanning 2019--2025 across Delhi-NCR monitoring stations, with the
graph hypothesis isolated behind a controlled ablation and judged with a
paired bootstrap rather than a per-seed standard deviation.

\section{Related work}
Spatiotemporal graph networks for forecasting were popularised by the diffusion
convolutional recurrent neural network of Li et al.\cite{dcrnn}, which placed a
recurrent model on a diffusion graph over road sensors and showed that capturing
spatial dependence improved over a convolutional baseline.  Yu et al.\cite{stgcn}
separated spatial and temporal operators into a spectral graph convolution
followed by gated temporal convolutions; the STGCN we train here is a GRU-based
variant of that architecture.  Graph WaveNet~\cite{graphwavenet} introduced
dilated causal convolutions with a fixed or learned adjacency.
On the air-quality side, spatiotemporal graph networks have been applied to
PM2.5 forecasting, typically with a distance-threshold graph, sometimes with a
wind-aware graph, reporting gains over graph-free baselines~\cite{pm25gnn}.
Two aspects of that literature motivate this study.  First, the reported gains
are usually measured against weak baselines, so a graph-free sequence model
can look competitive.  Second, the comparison is rarely made with enough
repetitions to resolve the small differences that graph structure actually
produces.

\section{Data}
\subsection{Source and coverage}
Hourly PM2.5 comes from the CPCB continuous monitoring network, obtained as
15-minute records for 2009--2025 from the \texttt{Vonter/india-cpcb-aqi}
release archive.  After resampling to hourly and dropping stations below 70\%
real-observation coverage in any split, the modelling set is 42 stations with
approximately 61{,}345 hourly timestamps over 2019-01-01 to 2025-12-31.
Station meteorology (temperature, relative humidity, wind speed and direction,
rainfall) is measured at the stations themselves.

\subsection{Splits and leakage control}
Splits are chronological and non-overlapping: train 2019--2022 (34{,}922
windows), validation 2023 (8{,}641 windows), test 2024--2025 (17{,}402
windows).  The split window counts sum to 60{,}965; the 23-hour gap that
separates the test block from validation accounts for the difference from the
61{,}345 total hours.  Every window's inputs \emph{and} targets lie wholly
inside its own split, verified programmatically.  The train and validation blocks are
contiguous; a 23-hour gap separates the test block from validation.
Normalisation ($\log(1{+}x)$ then z-score) is fitted on the training split
only, every graph is constructed from training-split information only, and
imputed hours carry a mask honoured in both the loss and the metrics.

\section{Methods}
\subsection{Baselines}
\textbf{Persistence} repeats the last observed value.
\textbf{Seasonal naive} repeats the value observed 24 hours before the target;
this is a far stronger baseline than plain persistence because Delhi's PM2.5
has a large diurnal cycle.
\textbf{Hour-of-week average} takes, for each station and hour of the week,
the mean observed PM2.5 over the training split.
\textbf{Ridge} fits a per-station linear map from the last 24 hourly values to
each horizon on the training split only.

\subsection{Models}
LSTM (univariate, shared weights per station; multivariate, stations as
channels), a temporal Transformer, STGCN (spectral graph convolution + GRU),
and Graph WaveNet (dilated causal convolutions with fixed or learned adjacency).
Fifteen configurations in total.

Two design choices matter more than architecture.  First, every network
predicts the \emph{change} from the last observed value and adds that value
back at the output; the output layer is zero-initialised, so each model
\emph{starts} at Persistence.  Second, each model carries a linear
autoregressive skip from the last 24 lags to the 48 horizons, so the network's
contribution is measured relative to a strong linear floor.

\paragraph{Training details.}
All models are trained with a masked Huber loss on the change from the last
observation ($\delta=1.0$), Adam (initial LR $10^{-3}$, cosine decay),
batch size 32, early stopping on validation MAE (patience 3).  The input
window is 72 hours and the output horizon is 48 hours.  Hyperparameters were
selected once on the 2023 validation block and held fixed.  Three random seeds
are used; the paired bootstrap resamples test weeks and uses the seed-average
per week, so seed variance is not double-counted.  About 24 paired comparisons
are reported; we do not apply a multiple-comparison correction.  The column
labelled $P(A{<}B)$ in the bootstrap tables is the fraction of bootstrap
replicates where A beats B, not a classical $p$-value.  A Bonferroni threshold
at $\alpha=0.05$ would require $P{<}0.002$; the headline contrasts (wind-aware
vs.\ distance for Graph WaveNet, and distance vs.\ no-graph for both
architectures) satisfy that threshold except STGCN distance vs.\ no-graph
($P=0.004$), which is treated as exploratory.

\subsection{Meteorology}
Weather is measured \emph{at} each station rather than taken from a reanalysis,
so the wind field carries genuine local structure: the mean cross-station
correlation of the u-component is 0.11, and no two stations record the same
wind in the same hour.  This matters for the graph experiment---a wind-aware
graph built on gridded reanalysis would have had almost nothing to add over a
distance graph.  It also introduces a caveat: a per-station \emph{mean} wind
direction estimated from weakly agreeing anemometers is itself a noisy quantity.

\subsection{Graphs}
Five structures are compared: \textbf{no graph} (identity, ablation baseline);
\textbf{distance}, Gaussian kernel $\exp(-d^2/2\sigma^2)$ on great-circle
distance, $\sigma=5$~km; \textbf{correlation}, pairwise-complete Pearson
correlation of PM2.5 on the training split, thresholded;
\textbf{wind-aware}, a directed edge $i\to j$ only when the prevailing wind at
$i$ points towards $j$: $\exp(-d^2/2\sigma^2)\cdot\max(0,\cos(\theta_{ij}-\phi_i))$,
a static graph oriented by the training-split period-mean direction;
\textbf{learned adaptive}, $\mathrm{softmax}(\mathrm{relu}(E_1E_2^\top))$,
learned in-model.

The five structures are compared across all configurations in Table~\ref{tab:ablation}.

The best neural model beats the best simple baseline at every horizon.  The
per-horizon curve of the \emph{baselines} is not monotone: the 12-hour column
is worse than the 24-hour one for persistence and ridge.  This is a property
of Delhi's PM2.5 data, which has a diurnal cycle with a peak-to-trough
amplitude of approximately 51~$\mu$g/m$^3$: a 12-hour horizon lands on the
opposite phase while a 24-hour horizon returns to the same phase.  Neural
models, which learn the diurnal structure, do not show this non-monotonicity.

\subsection{Is the null result caused by the wind speed?}

Windows binned by mean wind speed over the input period show that the no-graph
variant is ahead in the two lower-wind thirds and essentially tied in the
windiest third.  The severe-episode analysis below explains why: severe
episodes are disproportionately concentrated under calm conditions (78.2\% of
the lowest-wind third contains a severe-episode hour, against 52.1\% and 49.0\%
in the windier thirds), and it is there that all graphs pay their largest penalty.

\subsection{Is the result an artefact of the train/test boundary?}

Because several real years are available, we also ran a rolling-origin
evaluation: fold $k$ trains on 2019 through year $Y$, validates on Q4 of $Y$
for early stopping, and tests on year $Y{+}1$.  The evaluation was run for
STGCN (all five folds, test years 2021--2025) and Graph WaveNet (folds testing
2021, 2023, 2025).  Note that two folds (testing 2024 and 2025) overlap with
the main test block and are therefore not fully independent.  The no-graph
variant was the outright winner in three of five STGCN folds and within a few
tenths in the rest.  For Graph WaveNet the wind-aware graph won two of three
folds, which is the opposite of the no-graph ordering on that architecture.
The rolling-origin result is therefore best read as showing that the STGCN
ranking is stable as the boundary moves, while the Graph WaveNet ranking is
less so; the main test-block result is the more reliable comparison because it
uses all three seeds and 105 weeks of data.

\subsection{Spatial error: who pays for the mixing?}

If graph structure carried information a station cannot see, the benefit should
be largest at the edge of the network.  The evidence goes the other way.
For STGCN, both graph types impose a larger penalty at peripheral stations
(beyond the median centroid distance of 10.9~km) than at central ones:
wind-aware $+$0.21 near vs.\ $+$0.72 far; distance $+$0.38 near vs.\ $+$0.78
far.  The Spearman correlation between centroid radius and per-station penalty
is $+$0.41 (wind) and $+$0.33 (distance).  For Graph WaveNet the wind-aware
pattern is similar ($-$0.10 near vs.\ $+$0.32 far, $r=+0.41$) but the
distance graph has a slightly larger penalty near the centre ($+$0.89 near
vs.\ $+$0.85 far, $r=+0.15$, indistinguishable from zero at 42 stations).
The STGCN pattern is consistent with peripheral stations losing most from
mixing; the Graph WaveNet distance result does not confirm this.

The self-adaptive adjacency that Graph WaveNet learns is essentially
uncorrelated with pairwise distance (Spearman $r=+0.07$, against $-1.00$ for
the distance kernel by construction) and with the prevailing-wind alignment
($r=-0.06$).  The network learns a structure that is neither the distance graph
nor the wind-aware graph, and that structure does not pay.
The correlation graph and the learned adaptive graph are the two worst
configurations.  The correlation graph (35.03~$\mu$g/m$^3$) is the worst
neural model overall, significantly worse than no graph for both architectures
(STGCN: $+$0.86, CI [0.27, 1.49]; GWN: $+$1.46, CI [0.70, 2.29]) and
worse than the distance graph for Graph WaveNet ($+$0.62, CI [0.15, 1.14]).
The learned adaptive graph ($+$1.09 vs.\ no graph, CI [0.37, 1.87]) is second
worst among GWN configurations.

\subsection{Is the null result caused by the noisy local wind?}

Nine of the 42 stations report no wind at all in the CPCB files.  For those
stations the wind-aware graph is built from the network-mean direction.
A natural objection is that the edge directions are therefore noisy.
ERA5 reanalysis is the opposite treatment: complete for every station,
spatially smooth (cross-station correlation 0.98), but on a coarse grid.
Replacing station wind with ERA5 wind, changing nothing else, isolates the
wind source as the only variable.

The wind source does not matter.  For STGCN, ERA5 wind and station wind are
indistinguishable ($\Delta=-0.14$, CI [$-$0.35, $+$0.07]).  For Graph WaveNet,
the difference is $+$0.19 (CI [$-$0.07, $+$0.45]).  Crucially, the ERA5
variants do not beat no graph either: STGCN ERA5 vs.\ no graph is
$+$0.31 (CI [$-$0.01, $+$0.64], $p=0.028$, marginal) and GWN ERA5 vs.\ no graph
is $+$0.38 (CI [$-$0.00, $+$0.78], $p=0.027$, also marginal).  So neither
wind source makes the graph significantly worse than no graph; but neither
makes it significantly better.

The scope of this statement is narrow: it tests one edge construction against
one alternative wind source.  It does not show that wind cannot help.  A
time-lagged advection kernel, a wind field at higher spatial resolution, or
gradient-driven edge weights are different constructions not tested here.

\subsection{Is the graph just delivering the regional signal?}

We train a no-graph model with the network-wide mean PM2.5 as an extra input
channel and compare.  It buys essentially nothing: 32.60 against 32.61 without
it, a paired difference of 0.01~$\mu$g/m$^3$ (CI [$-$0.02, $+$0.00]), 0.03\%
of the score.  And the same model is significantly better than every graph
model (0.46 better than STGCN wind-aware, CI [0.16, 0.79]; 0.97 better than
GWN without a graph, CI [0.74, 1.20]).  The graph is not delivering the
regional signal and being ignored for it; delivering the regional signal
explicitly is worth essentially nothing.

\subsection{Can a graph help a station the model has never seen?}

Ten stations are held out entirely from training.  Their targets never enter
the training loss.  Two evaluation variants are compared:
\emph{mode~target}: the station's own 72-hour history is still visible but its
targets are hidden; \emph{mode~both}: both the targets and the station's own
history are hidden, so the only information available comes from neighbours.

In mode~target the picture is the same as the main experiment: the no-graph
variant is best (STGCN identity 35.35~$\mu$g/m$^3$) and both graph variants
are worse, though with wide confidence intervals (wind vs.\ no-graph:
$+$0.39, CI [$-$0.04, $+$0.85]; distance vs.\ no-graph: $+$0.59,
CI [$-$0.01, $+$1.22]).  These models do generalise to new monitors:
STGCN identity beats a ridge fitted on other stations by 12.1~$\mu$g/m$^3$
and seasonal naive by 6.1.

In mode~both the honest comparator is not the no-graph model (which has
nothing to work with and collapses, $R^2=-0.32$) but the simplest spatial
baselines that use the same information the graph has.  An
inverse-distance-weighted average of the neighbours' values from 24 hours
before the target (IDW-seasonal, 41.24~$\mu$g/m$^3$, $R^2=0.51$) beats the
best graph model (STGCN distance, 54.32~$\mu$g/m$^3$) by 13.1~$\mu$g/m$^3$
(CI [8.69, 18.04]).  This comparison has a known handicap: these models were
trained to forecast a station \emph{from its own history} and are now asked to
forecast one whose history has been removed---a condition they never practised,
while the IDW baseline has no such dependence.  The honest claim is therefore
the narrow one: graph models trained for standard forecasting do not transfer
to missing-history stations as well as a two-line distance weighting.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What the graph comparison shows}

All five graph structures were trained and evaluated identically, with 3 seeds,
and judged with a paired bootstrap over the 105 ISO weeks of the test period.
The differences are statistically clear but small in absolute terms:
between graph structures the gap reaches 1.1--1.5~$\mu$g/m$^3$ (3--4.5\%),
and the range from best (no graph, 32.61) to worst configuration
(GWN correlation, 35.03) is 2.4~$\mu$g/m$^3$ (7\%).

The picture is specific rather than a blanket negative.  For Graph WaveNet
the wind-aware graph is significantly better than the static distance graph:
33.76 against 34.39~$\mu$g/m$^3$, difference 0.63, CI [0.36, 0.91].
For STGCN the same comparison is inconclusive: 33.06 against 33.18, difference
0.12, CI [$-$0.15, 0.41].

What holds for both architectures is that every graph type is significantly
worse than no graph, or at best indistinguishable from it.  The distance graph
is significantly worse (STGCN $+$0.57, CI [0.14, 1.01]; GWN $+$0.82,
CI [0.36, 1.30]).  The wind-aware graph is also significantly worse than no
graph for STGCN ($+$0.45, CI [0.15, 0.79]); for Graph WaveNet the difference
($+$0.19, CI [$-$0.15, 0.55]) is not significant.  The correlation graph is the
worst configuration overall (GWN 35.03, $+$1.46 vs.\ no graph, CI [0.70, 2.29];
STGCN 33.47, $+$0.86, CI [0.27, 1.49]).  The learned adaptive adjacency is the
second worst GWN configuration ($+$1.09, CI [0.37, 1.87]).

The ERA5 ablation shows the wind source is not the issue.  Neither wind source
makes the graph significantly worse than no graph (both CIs include zero at the
margins), and neither makes it significantly better.  The null result is not an
artefact of the noisy or missing local wind.

Two diagnostics sharpen the interpretation.  The network-mean diagnostic shows
the graph is not delivering the regional signal (0.01~$\mu$g/m$^3$ explicitly
vs.\ the graph's mixing cost of 0.19--0.82).  The leave-station-out result
shows that in the regime designed to favour graph structure---a station with no
history of its own---a graph neural network trained without node dropout does
not match simple spatial interpolation; it is 13.1~$\mu$g/m$^3$ worse than
IDW-seasonal.

Our answer to the research question is narrow but defensible.  A wind-aware
graph beats a static distance graph for Graph WaveNet and is indistinguishable
from it for STGCN.  In both architectures, every graph type is either
significantly worse than no graph or indistinguishable from it; none is
significantly better.  The wind-aware graph is significantly worse than no
graph for STGCN (0.45, CI [0.15, 0.79]) and not significantly different for
Graph WaveNet (0.19, CI [$-$0.15, 0.55]).  The correlation graph and the
learned adaptive adjacency are the two worst configurations.  Two diagnostics
say why: the regional-signal diagnostic shows the graph's mixing cost
(0.19--1.46~$\mu$g/m$^3$ across graph types and architectures) is not
delivering a useful signal; and the leave-station-out result shows the graph
trained without node dropout cannot match simple spatial interpolation in the
missing-history regime.

\subsection{Failure analysis}

Three failure modes account for most of the residual error.  First, abrupt
anthropogenic spikes (Diwali, crop-residue burning) are not predictable from
meteorology or neighbouring stations, and every model smooths them away.
Second, sensor outages produce stale readings that the mask channel at least
flags.  Third, severe episodes are under-predicted: the model's autoregressive
signal is a poor guide when concentrations are building non-linearly, which is
why episode error is roughly 3.3$\times$ the non-episode error for every
architecture.

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item The test block covers two winters; generalisation across pollution
    regimes is only partly tested.
  \item The wind-aware graph is \emph{static}: it uses a period-mean prevailing
    direction, not a time-varying one.  A dynamic wind-aware graph is a
    different construction not tested here.
  \item The distance kernel uses $\sigma=5$~km against a median station spacing
    of $\sim$16~km, which makes the kernel nearly diagonal.  A larger-$\sigma$
    or $k$-nearest-neighbour distance graph is a different construction.
  \item Nine of the 42 stations report no wind at all.  The ERA5 ablation shows
    this does not drive the result, but it limits the precision of the
    station-specific wind direction used to orient the graph.
  \item ``Dilution of the autoregressive signal'' is a hypothesis consistent
    with the ablation, the network-mean diagnostic, and the leave-station-out
    result, but is not directly demonstrated.
  \item Single region, single hazard; results may not transfer to other cities
    or pollutants.
  \item Graph structures are static except Graph WaveNet's learned variant.
  \item Leave-station-out models were trained without node dropout; the
    missing-history comparison therefore contains a known handicap.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

Across seven years of real observations at 42 stations, every neural model we
trained beats ridge regression and persistence.  Most beat seasonal naive; the
multivariate LSTM is modestly better ($-$1.91~$\mu$g/m$^3$, CI [$-$3.60,
$-$0.05], about 5\%), and the Transformer is worse ($+$0.18).  The best model
beats the best simple baseline by 15.6\% in aggregate (32.61 vs.\ 38.63,
CI [4.82, 7.37]) and by a positive margin at every horizon.

The graph question has a more specific answer than a simple negative.  A
wind-aware directed graph beats a static distance graph for Graph WaveNet
(0.63~$\mu$g/m$^3$, CI [0.36, 0.91] by paired bootstrap) and is
indistinguishable from it for STGCN (0.12, CI [$-$0.15, 0.41]).
In both architectures, every graph type is either significantly worse than no
graph or indistinguishable from it.  The wind-aware graph is significantly
worse than no graph for STGCN (0.45, CI [0.15, 0.79]) and not significantly
different for Graph WaveNet (0.19, CI [$-$0.15, 0.55]).
The correlation graph (35.03) and the learned adaptive adjacency (34.66) are
the two worst GWN configurations; the correlation graph is the worst neural
model overall.

Two diagnostics explain why.  Handing a no-graph model the network-wide mean
PM2.5 gains essentially nothing (0.01~$\mu$g/m$^3$), so the graph is not
delivering a useful regional signal.  And on a station the model has never seen
and whose history is missing, a graph neural network trained without node
dropout is 13.1~$\mu$g/m$^3$ worse than inverse-distance-weighted interpolation
--- not because the graph is useless, but because the models were trained for
the regime in which a station's own history is available.

The consistent story is that the per-station autoregressive signal is what
matters, and inter-station mixing at this station density provides at best a
small benefit (wind-aware over distance for Graph WaveNet) and at worst a net
cost (distance graph in both architectures).  The remaining error is
concentrated almost entirely in severe pollution episodes.  Whether a
differently constructed graph pays off on a denser network, a finer temporal
resolution, or a longer and more diverse dataset is left open.
